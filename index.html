Home/Exterior/Waterproofing & Drainage

Keep Water Out.
Permanently.

What This Is

Water Finds Every
Crack You Ignore.

A wet basement or water-infiltrated foundation wall doesn't dry out on its own. It gets worse — and it takes your framing, insulation, and home value down with it. VWG diagnoses where water is coming from and solves it at the source.

We install interior and exterior waterproofing systems, French drains, sump pumps, and surface drainage solutions. We don't mask the problem with a coat of paint.

What's Included

  • Foundation crack injection & sealing
  • Exterior membrane waterproofing
  • Interior drainage systems (channel & sump)
  • French drain installation
  • Sump pump installation & backup
  • Window well drainage
  • Grading corrections
  • Downspout extensions
Our Process

Find It. Fix It. Forget It.

01

Diagnostic Assessment

We find where the water is actually coming from — not just where it's showing up.

02

Source Treatment

Cracks sealed, exterior membrane applied, or exterior excavation for severe cases.

03

Drainage System

Interior channel, sump pump, or French drain installed to manage ongoing water movement.

04

Grade & Surface

Ground graded away from foundation. Downspouts extended. Problem solved long-term.

5 Signs Your Roof Needs
More Than a Patch Job

By Henry Valencia  ·  June 2025  ·  5 min read

Most homeowners wait too long. Here's how to know when a repair becomes a replacement — and why waiting costs you more in the long run.

Sign 1: Curling or Missing Shingles

When shingles start curling at the edges or corners, they've lost their bond with the layer beneath. That's not a surface problem — it's a structural one. Missing shingles leave your decking exposed to every rain event. One good storm while you're waiting for a "better time to deal with it" can turn a $3,000 roof repair into a $15,000 interior water damage job.

Sign 2: Granule Loss in Your Gutters

Asphalt shingles are coated with granules that protect them from UV degradation. When you start finding dark, sand-like grit in your gutters and downspout splash zones, your shingles are breaking down. This typically signals a roof that's in its final five years of useful life. New granule loss means the clock is ticking.

Sign 3: Daylight Through the Attic Boards

Grab a flashlight and get in your attic on a bright day. If you see daylight poking through the roof boards — even pinhole light — water is already finding those same entry points. This is past the patch-job stage. You're looking at full replacement with new decking.

Sign 4: Sagging Sections

A sagging roof is never a cosmetic issue. It means the decking or the structural supports beneath it are compromised — usually from long-term moisture intrusion. This is an emergency repair, not something to schedule for next season. A sagging roof can fail completely under snow load or heavy rain.

Sign 5: The Roof Is Over 20 Years Old

Most asphalt shingle roofs have a functional life of 20–25 years. If yours is in that range, even if it "looks okay," it's running on borrowed time. An inspection will tell you how much life is left. A good contractor will give you an honest answer — not just tell you what gets them the job.

What to Do Next

If you're seeing two or more of these signs, call for an inspection before anything else. A proper roof inspection takes 30–45 minutes and gives you a clear picture of what you're actually dealing with. VWG does these at no charge for homeowners in our service area.

Kitchen Remodel vs. Kitchen Refresh:
Which One Do You Actually Need?

By Henry Valencia  ·  June 2025  ·  6 min read

Not every kitchen needs a gut renovation. But some refreshes end up costing more than a remodel. We break it down so you can make the right call.

What a Kitchen Refresh Covers

A refresh is surface-level work: new cabinet doors and hardware, new countertops, new appliances, a fresh coat of paint, maybe a new backsplash. You're keeping the existing layout, the existing cabinet boxes, the existing plumbing and electrical runs. A good refresh can transform the look of a kitchen for $8,000–$20,000 depending on materials.

The catch: if your cabinet boxes are damaged, if the layout doesn't work for how you actually cook, or if the plumbing is in the wrong place — a refresh is putting lipstick on a problem.

What a Full Remodel Covers

A remodel starts at demolition. Everything comes out: cabinets, counters, flooring, sometimes the drywall. You're redesigning the layout, moving plumbing and electrical if needed, and building the kitchen from scratch with new everything. Range: $30,000–$80,000+ depending on size and finishes.

The benefit: you fix everything that was wrong, get the layout you actually want, and end up with a kitchen that functions well for the next 20 years.

The Cost Trap to Avoid

Here's where homeowners get stuck: they spend $15,000 on a refresh — new counters, new doors, new appliances — and then two years later realize the layout still doesn't work and they still hate being in the kitchen. Now they have to remodel anyway, but they wasted $15,000 first.

If the layout is the problem, start with the remodel. If the layout works and you just want it to look better, a refresh is the right call.

How to Decide

Ask yourself two questions: Does the layout work for how we cook? And: Are the cabinet boxes structurally sound? If both answers are yes, a refresh will serve you well. If either answer is no, budget for the full remodel now rather than a refresh now and a remodel later.

What Happens During a Home Addition?
A Week-by-Week Breakdown

By Henry Valencia  ·  June 2025  ·  7 min read

Adding a room to your house is exciting. It can also feel chaotic if you don't know what to expect. Here's what the process actually looks like — week by week.

Planning Week: Nothing Gets Built Without a Plan

Before the first shovel goes in the ground, your contractor is doing design work, filing permit applications, and ordering materials with long lead times. In Westchester and the surrounding area, permits for additions can take 3–6 weeks depending on the municipality. This is not a process you can rush. Use this time to finalize your finish selections — you'll need to know what you're putting in before we can design what's holding it up.

Permits Week: The Government's Turn

Permit approval triggers the construction start. Your contractor should pull permits — not you, and not an unlicensed crew. Permits mean inspections, and inspections mean the work is checked. A contractor who tells you permits aren't necessary is telling you they plan to build something that won't pass inspection.

Foundation Week: The Ground Truth

Excavation, formwork, and concrete pour. For most additions, this is a stem wall or slab — 1–2 weeks of work that needs to cure properly before anything goes on top of it. This is also when underground utilities get confirmed. Surprises happen here. A good contractor budgets time for them.

Framing Week: The Shape Appears

This is the week that feels like magic. The framing crew turns lumber into the shape of your addition in 3–5 days. Walls, floor system, roof framing — it all happens fast and your neighbors start asking questions. Enjoy it. This is where the project looks best before it gets complicated.

Finishing Weeks: The Long Middle

After framing comes the long list: roofing over the addition, windows and doors, sheathing, insulation, drywall, mechanical rough-ins, tape and mud, flooring, paint, trim. This phase takes 3–6 weeks depending on scope. It's less visually dramatic but it's where 80% of the quality is determined.

Final Walkthrough: Your Job Is to Find Problems

Walk the space with your contractor before you close out the contract. Bring a flashlight. Check corners, check grout lines, open every door and window twice. A good contractor wants you to find the punch list items now — not six months after they've left.

HVAC

Why Westchester Homeowners
Are Switching to Ductless AC

By Henry Valencia  ·  June 2025  ·  5 min read

Central air isn't the only option anymore. Ductless mini-split systems are quieter, more efficient, and easier to install than you think.

What Is a Ductless System?

A ductless mini-split system has two main components: an outdoor compressor and one or more indoor air handlers. Unlike central air, there's no ductwork. Each indoor unit conditions one zone — a room or floor — independently. The connection between inside and outside is a small hole in the wall for the refrigerant line set and electrical.

Why It Works Better for Older Homes

Most Westchester homes were built before central air was standard. Adding ductwork means significant demolition — opening walls, ceilings, and floors to route ducts through a structure that wasn't designed for them. Ductless systems solve this entirely. No ductwork means no demolition. An installation that would take 3–4 weeks with central air takes 1–2 days with ductless.

The Efficiency Advantage

Duct systems lose 20–30% of their conditioned air to leakage before it reaches the rooms you're trying to cool. Mini-splits eliminate that loss entirely. A quality ductless system operates at a SEER rating of 18–25+, compared to 14–18 for a standard central system. The difference shows up in your electric bill every month.

Zone Control: The Overlooked Benefit

A multi-zone ductless system lets each room control its own temperature independently. No more arguments about the thermostat. No more conditioning rooms that nobody is using. If you work from home in one room, you can cool that room without running the system for the whole house.

Best Candidates for Ductless

If you're in a home without existing ductwork, a home addition, a finished basement or attic, a garage or workshop, or a room that your central system doesn't reach well — ductless is almost always the right answer. For new construction with ductwork already planned, central air remains a strong option.

The Truth About Getting
Multiple Contractor Quotes

By Henry Valencia  ·  June 2025  ·  6 min read

Everyone says get three quotes. But what are you actually comparing? Price, timeline, or who showed up and listened? Here's what to look for — and what to watch out for.

What Quotes Don't Tell You

A quote is a number on paper. It doesn't tell you who is actually going to show up to your house. It doesn't tell you whether the contractor has licensed workers or subcontracts everything to the lowest bidder. It doesn't tell you what their cleanup habits are, how they handle change orders, or whether they'll be responsive when something unexpected comes up at 4pm on a Friday.

Red Flags Worth Walking Away From

No written contract. Cash-only payment structure. A price that's 40% lower than the other quotes. No ability to provide proof of insurance. Vague scope of work with no material specifications. A quote delivered in 10 minutes without measuring anything. Any one of these should make you pause. Two or more should end the conversation.

Green Flags That Signal the Right Contractor

Pulls their own permits. Can explain what's in the quote line by line. Has real references you can call — not just a list of names. Shows up on time to the estimate appointment. Asks you questions before they start talking numbers. Gives you a realistic timeline, not the one you want to hear.

What Questions to Ask

Ask who will actually be working on your home — the estimator's crew, or a subcontractor they called last week. Ask how they handle unexpected conditions — like rot under siding or plumbing that's not where it should be. Ask how they communicate during the project. Ask what happens if you're not satisfied with a portion of the work.

Why Relationships Matter More Than Price

The cheapest quote almost never delivers the cheapest outcome. That $8,000 discount often comes back as cost overruns, material substitutions, and a final product that isn't what you agreed to. The contractor who communicates clearly, shows up when they say they will, and stands behind their work is worth the extra money every single time. You're not just buying materials and labor — you're buying the experience of working with someone who gives a damn about your home.
